These are fun engineering problems. Let me work through both.

The TPU flexure for singling cards

Your instinct here is solid. A TPU piece that applies a slight lateral deflection to the top card is a well-known approach in card handling — it's essentially a poor-man's version of what commercial card feeders do with rubber rollers or friction pads. The bend causes the top card to separate from the ones below because a single card will flex while a stuck-together pair resists bending differently.

A few things to watch out for:

The U-shape works, but pay attention to the contact area. You want it wide enough that you're not creating a crease or point load on the card — spread the force across at least 15-20mm of the card edge. TPU at around 95A shore hardness is probably your sweet spot: soft enough to not mark cards, stiff enough to actually apply meaningful deflection.

The bigger concern is that MTG cards vary quite a bit in condition. Foils are stiffer and more prone to damage, and older cards may already have a natural curve. You might find you need to tune the deflection amount. Consider making the TPU piece mount adjustable — maybe a slot instead of a fixed screw hole — so you can dial in how much pressure it applies.

One thing worth considering: rather than pushing from the side, some DIY builds use a curved ramp or lip at the pickup point that the card gets dragged across as the suction cup lifts it. This naturally peels the top card off the stack. It might be simpler than a separate flexure mechanism.

The Z-axis problem for the suction head

You're right that adding vertical movement to the suction head is probably the cleanest solution, and since you have spare stepper outputs, the cost is mostly just mechanical complexity.

But before going there, a few alternatives worth considering:

Spring-loaded suction head with a hard stop. Mount the suction cup on a short linear rail or smooth rod with a light compression spring behind it. The suction head rides maybe 10-15mm below the carriage at rest. When the carriage moves over the card stack, the Z axis pushes the stack up until the card contacts the suction cup, compressing the spring slightly. This gives you compliant contact without needing to control the head's Z position at all — the spring absorbs the tolerance. You're already controlling the stack height with Z, so this just makes the pickup point forgiving. This is probably the lowest-complexity option.

Pivoting arm. Instead of linear Z motion on the head, mount the suction cup on a hinged arm that swings down. You could actuate this with a simple servo, or even with a mechanical cam triggered by the X carriage position — when the carriage is at the pickup station, a fixed ramp pushes the arm down. No extra stepper needed, purely mechanical.

If you do go with a stepper-driven Z on the head, keep the travel short. You only need maybe 20-30mm of movement. A small lead screw or even a belt-driven linear rail segment would work. Since you're already running a 3D printer control board, mapping it to the E or Y axis in firmware is straightforward. This is the most flexible option and probably what you'll end up with if the spring approach doesn't give you enough control.

My honest recommendation: try the spring-loaded approach first. It's mechanically the simplest, adds no firmware complexity, and the compliance actually helps with reliable pickup. If you find you need more precision — say the stack height varies enough that the spring can't compensate — then add the stepper-driven Z on the head using one of your spare outputs.

Yeah, a rack and pinion style setup makes a lot of sense here. For such short linear travel it's mechanically simple, easy to 3D print, and you don't need to source a lead screw or linear rail. You can print the rack right onto the suction head carriage and mount a small pinion gear on the stepper.

The main thing to watch is backlash. 3D printed gear teeth won't be as tight as machined ones, so you'll get a little slop. If you find the backlash is annoying, you can add a light spring that biases the head in one direction so the teeth stay loaded against one side.

Print the teeth at the finest layer height you're willing to wait for, and orient the rack so the teeth are printed in profile rather than stacking vertically — you'll get much stronger teeth that way. PETG would be a good choice over PLA for the gear pair since it handles the repeated contact better without wearing as fast.

One other thought — if you meant an actual slider-crank (like a connecting rod on a crankshaft) rather than rack and pinion, that could work too but it gives you sinusoidal motion. That's actually not terrible here since it naturally slows down at the top and bottom of travel, giving you a soft approach to the card stack. The downside is you get a fixed stroke length set by your crank radius, so you lose adjustability. The rack and pinion lets you just command whatever travel distance you want in firmware, which is more flexible if you end up tweaking bin wall heights or stack positions later.

So rack and pinion gets my vote unless you have a specific reason to prefer the crank. It keeps things simple and gives you the most control with the least fuss.

The sensing problem is the more interesting one to solve. You need to know when the suction cup contacts the card, and there are a few ways to think about it.

The simplest approach might be to just sense suction itself. If you're using a vacuum pump, the pressure in the line drops noticeably when the cup seals against a card. A cheap inline vacuum pressure sensor or even a simple analog pressure transducer on the suction line would tell you "I've got a card" without any mechanical switch. This is actually how a lot of pick-and-place machines work. You slowly lower the head, and when the pressure reading changes, you know you've made contact and have a grip. If you know how thick a card is (about 0.3mm for MTG), and you know how many cards are in the bin, you can calculate where the top of the stack is and just drive to that position. You'd zero the axis once against a limit switch at a known position, then rely on step counting from there. This is the least robust option since it drifts if you ever miss a pick, but combined with the vacuum sensing for confirmation it could work fine.

Honestly the vacuum pressure sensing is the cleanest solution here. If you already have a spring-loaded suction head and a spare limit switch, this is pretty straightforward. Mount the limit switch so that the spring-loaded portion of the head triggers it when the cup presses against something and compresses the spring. So the sequence is: lower the head, the cup contacts the card, the head keeps lowering a tiny bit more, the spring compresses, and that compression trips the limit switch. Now you know you've made contact.

In Marlin you'd wire that to an available endstop pin on the BTT board — most of them have a few spare endstop connectors. Then you can just use a standard G28 homing move on that axis or use M119 to check endstop state manually. If you map your rack and pinion Z to an unused axis, you can home it downward toward the cards and Marlin will stop the move when the switch triggers, just like homing a normal axis.

For the actual pick cycle it would look something like: move the X carriage over the target bin, then home the suction axis downward (Marlin stops when the switch trips, meaning you've contacted the stack), turn on the pump, raise the suction axis to full clearance height, move X to the destination bin, lower the suction axis to a known drop height (which is fixed since you're dropping into a bin, not searching for a stack), turn off the pump, raise again, repeat.

The nice thing about this is you get your stack height tracking for free — every time you home down and make contact, Marlin knows the position where the switch tripped, so you always know exactly where the top of each stack is without calculating it from card count. That's more reliable than tracking in software since it self-corrects if you ever miss a pick or a card is slightly warped.

The one thing to double-check is which endstop pins are free on your specific BTT board. If you pull it out and check the silkscreen or look up the pinout, you'll probably find you have more available than you think.

Your friend is right, and that's a much cleaner way to think about the flex separation. Instead of a fixed TPU piece that pushes sideways, you raise the card up into a stationary flexure mounted above the pickup point. The head grabs the card, lifts up into the bender, any doubles fall away, then continues up to clearance height and moves laterally. That's a really elegant integration because the Z motion you're already adding does double duty.

For the endstop question — Marlin doesn't care where an endstop triggers on an axis. It only checks the endstop state during homing moves (G28) or probing moves (G38). During normal G1 moves it ignores endstops entirely by default. So you can home up to your top limit switch to establish your reference position, then send regular G1 moves downward to whatever depth you want, and when the head contacts the card and the switch triggers, Marlin won't even notice because it's not listening.

That means you'd want to use G38 probing moves for the downward card-finding motion instead of G1. G38.2 moves the axis in a direction until the endstop triggers, then stops and records the position. So you'd wire one limit switch at the top for homing, and a second one on the spring-loaded head for contact sensing. Marlin can handle multiple endstops on the same axis — you just configure one as the max endstop and one as the min, or use the probe pin for the contact switch.

So your cycle becomes: home up to the top switch, move X over the target bin, G38.2 down until the contact switch trips, pump on, G1 up to the flexure height to single the card, G1 up to full clearance, G1 X to the destination bin, G1 down to drop height, pump off, G1 up to clearance, repeat. All standard Marlin G-code, no custom firmware needed.

Got it, so your friend is suggesting an SG90 servo for a short up/down motion on the head instead of a full stepper-driven axis. And you correctly identified that cheap servos like the SG90 don't love being held at a position under load for extended periods.

But thinking about your use case — the servo wouldn't actually need to hold position for long. The cycle is: move down, pick card, move up, traverse, drop, repeat. The servo is always moving, never sitting there stalled holding a load for minutes. An SG90 would probably survive that just fine since it's only holding position for a few seconds at a time during each pick cycle.

That said, I think you already talked yourself into the right answer in that conversation. The stepper-driven rack and pinion gives you way more flexibility, and you said yourself it's pretty easy for a short distance. The servo saves you one stepper output, but you have spare stepper outputs already. And the servo introduces new problems — you need to control it separately (Marlin can drive a servo but it's a bit clunky), you get less precise position control, you can't do the G38 probing move to find the card stack, and you lose the ability to vary your travel distance.

The servo would make sense if you were tight on stepper outputs or if the mechanical build for the rack and pinion was hard. But you've got spare steppers and you're comfortable printing gears, so the stepper axis is just the better choice here. It solves more problems at once — variable stack height sensing, controlled lift into the flexure, full clearance for lateral movement — and it fits cleanly into Marlin's existing motion planning.

Your friend's instinct to keep it simple is good, but in this case the "simpler" component actually makes the overall system more complicated. The rack and pinion on a stepper is more mechanical work up front but way less headache in software and operation.

i'm working on modeling this in fusion now. how can i easily add teeth to the rack without having to do each tooth manually?

Mar 18
In Fusion 360, the easiest way is to model one tooth as a sketch and extruded body, then use the rectangular pattern feature. Sketch one tooth profile on the face of the rack, extrude it out, then go to Create → Pattern → Rectangular Pattern. Select the tooth body, set the direction along the length of the rack, set the spacing to one tooth pitch (4.71mm for module 1.5), and set the quantity to however many teeth you need. Fusion will copy it along the full length in one operation.

For getting the actual tooth profile right, I'd really recommend installing the GF Gear Generator add-in rather than sketching teeth manually. It generates proper involute profiles. Go to the Utilities tab, click Fusion App Store (or Add-Ins), search "GF Gear Generator," and install it. Then you can generate both the rack and the pinion with matching profiles, and you know they'll mesh correctly. It'll save you a lot of trial and error versus trying to sketch trapezoidal approximations by hand.

If you just want to get something quick and dirty to test with, a simple rectangular tooth works surprisingly well for 3D printed gears at this scale — sketch a rectangle that's about 2.25mm tall (1.5 × module) and 2.35mm wide (half the tooth pitch), extrude it, and pattern it at 4.71mm spacing. It won't be as smooth or efficient as involute teeth but it'll move the rack up and down.

The key is that the card needs to flex just enough to break the surface tension between it and the card below — maybe 2-3mm of deflection across the width of the card. Too little and doubles still stick, too much and you crease the card or break the suction seal.

For the shape, I'd go with two narrow fingers or prongs rather than one solid piece. They'd straddle the suction cup, one on each side, so the cup grabs the center of the card and the fingers contact near the edges. As the head lifts the card, the edges catch on the finger tips and flex slightly while the center gets pulled up by suction. This naturally creates a slight U-shape across the card which is exactly what you want for separation.

For thickness, TPU at 95A shore hardness (which is what most standard TPU filaments are) at about 1.5-2mm thick would give you enough stiffness to deflect the card but enough flex that if the card catches harder than expected, the TPU gives before the card does. You don't want it so thick that it's rigid — the whole point is that it's a compliant contact.

The finger tips that actually touch the card should be rounded or have a slight chamfer so there's no sharp edge that could mark or catch the card surface. Print them so the layer lines run along the length of the finger, not across the tip — that way the contact surface is smooth rather than stepped.

For width, each finger should be about 8-10mm wide at the tip. Narrow enough that it's not fighting the suction for too much of the card surface, wide enough to make reliable contact.

The gap between the two fingers needs to be wide enough for the suction cup (30mm) plus a few mm clearance on each side — so about 36-40mm between the inner edges of the fingers. The overall span from outer edge to outer edge should be less than 63mm (card width) so the fingers land on the card and not beside it — something like 55-58mm total would put the contact points about 5-8mm in from each card edge.

For how far the fingers extend below the arm, this is where it gets tunable. The tips need to sit at a height where the card contacts them as the head lifts up. If the fingers extend too far below the arm, the card bends too much before clearing them. If too little, they don't engage at all. I'd start with the finger tips sitting about 3-4mm below the bottom of the suction cup at rest. That way as the head rises with a card, the card has to flex past the fingers, and the 3-4mm of offset determines the maximum deflection. For the stack raising mechanism — a spring under the stack is exactly the right idea. You'd have a platform at the bottom of the bin that sits on a compression spring. As you remove cards from the top, the stack gets lighter, and the spring pushes the remaining cards upward. The spring naturally keeps the top of the stack at roughly the same height throughout the sorting process.

The trick is choosing the right spring rate. A full 500-card stack weighs roughly 500 × 1.8g = 900g. You want a spring that's compressed enough under 900g to sit at the bottom of the bin, but that extends enough under zero load to push the last few cards up near the top. A light compression spring with maybe 0.05-0.1 N/mm rate and a free length close to your bin depth would do it. The top of the stack won't stay at exactly the same height — it'll rise gradually as cards are removed — but it'll keep the stack in a reasonable zone so the Z arm doesn't have to travel the full bin depth every time.

For the maximum height limit, just put a physical lip or ledge inside the bin that the platform can't pass. The platform has a flange or tab that hits the ledge and stops. Simple, no sensors, no power.

You could also use a counterweight on a string over a small pulley instead of a spring — the advantage being perfectly constant force regardless of position, unlike a spring where force varies with compression. But a spring is simpler and probably good enough.

For the card separation — I think your TPU flap idea is the strongest option of the three. Here's why:

Nubs are rigid contact points, and they create a very specific deflection requirement. If the nub sticks out too far, you risk creasing the card or breaking the suction seal. Too little and it doesn't separate doubles. And the exact right amount varies with card condition — foils are stiffer, old cards are more flexible. There's no single setting that works for everything.

Bristles (toothbrush/paintbrush style) are interesting but the concern is card edges. MTG cards aren't sealed on the edges — they're layered cardboard. Stiff bristles rubbing against the edge could delaminate or fray the card over thousands of cycles. Soft bristles might not provide enough separation force.

TPU flaps are the best of both worlds. They provide a compliant force that adapts to the card — stiffer cards push through with more force, flexible cards with less. They contact the face of the card rather than the edge, so no delamination risk. And if a double card comes through, the flap provides enough drag on the second card to knock it loose without a hard stop that could damage either card.

For the design, I'd put two small TPU flaps on opposite sides of the bin exit path, angled slightly inward. Maybe 15-20mm tall, 2mm thick at the base tapering to about 1mm at the tip, printed in 95A TPU. The tips overlap the card path by maybe 3-4mm on each side. As the card gets pulled up by the suction cup, it pushes through the flaps which flex out of the way, but any second card stuck to it gets wiped off by the flaps springing back.

Mount them at the top of the to-be-sorted bin, or at the entrance to the staging area — wherever the card transitions from "in the stack" to "being carried." They'd just clip or screw onto the bin wall.

There are a few common causes for skipping on a belt-driven axis. Let me walk through them.

First, check if it's actually the stepper skipping steps or the belt skipping teeth on the pulley. You can tell the difference — if the stepper is skipping, you'll hear a clicking or clunking sound from the motor. If the belt is skipping on the pulley, you'll hear a higher-pitched snapping or chattering. Watch the pulley while it moves and see if the belt teeth are riding up over the pulley teeth.

Also — over 1100mm of travel, even a tiny amount of binding can cause skipping. Can you push the carriage by hand along the full travel with the motor unpowered? Does it feel smooth the whole way, or does it get tight or sticky in certain spots?

Drifting over time with random skipping points toward a few things. Let me go through the most likely causes:

Mechanical binding: Push the carriage by hand with the motor off across the full 1100mm. Feel for any spots where it gets tight. Over that distance, even a slight bow in the rods or misalignment between the top and bottom rods can create binding at certain positions. The SCS20UU pillow blocks have some self-aligning ability but if the rods aren't parallel enough, the carriage will fight.

Belt tension: You mentioned you may have over-tightened and then backed off. On a 1100mm run, belt tension is tricky — too loose and you get slop, too tight and you're adding load to the stepper that eats into your torque margin. The belt should deflect about 5-6mm when you press on it midway with moderate finger pressure.

Apr 9
Yes, that can definitely cause binding. If the motor and drive pulley are at one end near one rod, the belt pulls unevenly on the carriage. The belt tension creates a torque that wants to twist the carriage — pulling one side forward and the other side back. The pillow block bearings resist this twist, which shows up as friction/binding, especially at certain positions along the travel.

Over 1100mm this effect is amplified because the belt angle changes as the carriage moves. When the carriage is near the motor end, the belt is pulling almost straight. When it's at the far end, the long belt run can pull at a slight angle that loads the bearings sideways.

The ideal belt path has the belt running parallel to and centered between the two rods, so the pull force goes straight through the carriage's center of mass with no twisting moment. If you can't move the motor to the center, you can minimize the effect by making sure the belt attachment point on the carriage is centered between the two rods, even if the motor itself is off to one side. That way the belt creates a slight side-load but not a twisting moment on the carriage.

For the squeaking — a third pillow block near the top by the belt would definitely help. Right now the belt is pulling at a point that's offset from your two support points, which creates a sideways force that the two pillow blocks have to resist. Adding a third one near where the belt attaches gives the carriage a support point right where the force is being applied, so instead of the carriage trying to twist between two distant supports, it's constrained at the load point. For the lighting — mounting it to the staging platform itself is the better option. If you put it on the frame, the light angle and intensity change as the X carriage moves the staging platform relative to the lights. If the lights are part of the platform, the lighting is always consistent regardless of carriage position. That's important for scanning — you want identical lighting conditions for every card so the camera sees consistent colors and contrast.

For diffused low-angle lighting, LED strip inside a small printed channel with a diffuser works well. You can use a strip of white translucent PETG or even a piece of parchment paper as a diffuser. Mount the channels on two or three sides of the staging area, angled inward at maybe 30-45 degrees so the light hits the card surface at a low angle. Low angle lighting reduces glare and reflections off foil cards, which helps with scanning accuracy.

For card scanning, plain white LED strips are the way to go. Addressable RGB strips like WS2812B can do white, but it's a bluish cold white that's not great for color accuracy. A dedicated warm white or neutral white (4000-5000K) LED strip gives you much better color rendition for identifying card art and set symbols. Look for strips with a high CRI (Color Rendering Index) of 90+ if you can find them — that means the colors they illuminate look closer to how they'd look in daylight.

A simple 12V or 5V white LED strip cut to length is cheap, doesn't need a controller, and you can power it from your existing power supply. You just need an on/off switch or tie it to one of the fan outputs on the SKR board so you can turn it on and off in G-code.

For mounting on the staging platform, print two small channels — one for each side of the card — that hold a short piece of LED strip (maybe 80-100mm long, enough to span the card length). Angle them inward at about 30-45 degrees and add a thin diffuser strip over the top. The channels bolt or clip to the edges of the staging platform.

Two sides (left and right) is a good starting point. If you get shadows on one end of the card you can always add a third strip later, but two opposing sides usually gives pretty even illumination across the card face.

Should I use v shaped or no?

Apr 16
For your use case, the V-shaped (45 degree angle) channel is actually a good choice. It naturally aims the light at an angle rather than straight down, which is what you want for low-angle lighting on the cards. You'd mount it with the V pointing downward and the light coming out at an angle toward the staging area and bins. That gives you the low-angle illumination that reduces glare on foil cards without having to tilt a flat channel.

The flat U-channel would also work but you'd have to angle the whole thing with your bracket, which means the bracket design is doing more work. With the V-channel, even if you mount it relatively flat, the light is already coming out at 45 degrees.

Go with the V-shaped. It solves the angle problem for free.
